The Courts of the Federation

Justice in the Federation is done by independent courts. The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal, the Constitutional Court decides whether laws are valid, and appellate and lower courts hear the cases between. Every State also keeps its own court system.

      Articles 18–20 and 27

      Five kinds of court

      The judicial power of the Federation is vested in the Supreme Court, the Constitutional Court, appellate courts and the lower federal courts. The States run courts of their own.

      What it decides
      Final word on
      Supreme Court
      Appeals in every case under federal law and the Constitution. It may strike down any law, regulation or executive act that breaks the Constitution. It hears disputes between States, between a State and the Federation, and cases involving foreign diplomats.
      Federal law and the Constitution, on appeal
      Constitutional Court
      Whether federal and State laws are valid, on challenge. It alone settles disputes about the division of powers between the Federation and the States, and may strike down a constitutional amendment that breaks the eternity clauses.
      Constitutional validity
      Appellate courts
      Appeals from the lower federal courts, in four circuits that group the States. Most federal appeals end here, and the Supreme Court takes the rest.
      Most federal appeals
      Lower federal courts
      Trials of federal offences, federal civil claims and review of federal agencies, with juries in criminal trials.
      Facts at trial
      State courts
      Everything within the States’ reserved powers: most crime, family and property law, local government and the great majority of everyday disputes. Each State has its own court system and constitution.
      State law

      How a case travels

      1. Trial

        In a State court or a lower federal court. A jury sits in criminal trials, and a public defender is provided to anyone who cannot pay.

      2. Appeal

        To the State’s higher courts, or to the appellate court of the federal circuit.

      3. Constitutional question

        Any court hearing a case in which the validity of a law is raised may refer it to the Constitutional Court.

      4. Supreme Court

        The final court of appeal under federal law. Its decisions bind every court and public authority.

      5. Judgment

        Judgments are given in public and published. Decisions of the Supreme Court bind every court and public authority.

      Articles 19–20

      The bench

      Sixteen judges sit on the two highest courts. Supreme Court justices serve until age 75. Constitutional Court judges serve one non-renewable twelve-year term, so that none can shape a ruling around a future career.

      Constitutional Court

      The seven terms end in different years, so the bench is never renewed all at once.

      Article 20

      Constitutional review

      The Constitutional Court is separate from the ordinary appeal courts, so that questions about the Constitution are decided by specialists in public law and not case by case.

      Who may petition

      The President
      On any federal or State law.
      The Cabinet
      By a reference from the government.
      The Assembly
      One third of either chamber, the House or the Senate.
      A State
      Any State government.
      A court
      Any court hearing a case in which constitutional validity is raised.

      What a ruling does

      Void
      A law struck down is void from the date of the ruling.
      Delayed
      The Court may delay the effect so that the Assembly can correct the law.
      Binding
      Its decisions bind all other courts and public bodies.
      Powers
      The Court alone settles disputes between the Federation and the States over the division of powers (Art. 28).
      Amendments
      It can strike down an amendment that breaks the eternity clauses of Article 54.

      Federal law may not tell a State government how to use its reserved powers. The Federation may set minimum standards, and States may exceed them but not fall below them (Art. 28).

      Articles 4, 8 and 9

      Your rights in court

      These rights are self-executing and enforceable before any court. Several can never be suspended, even in an emergency (Art. 32).

      A fair, public and speedy trial

      Before an independent court, with the presumption of innocence. No one may be tried twice for the same offence or under a law made after the act.

      A lawyer, whatever you earn

      Free access to the courts and adequate legal assistance may not be denied for poverty. The state provides public defenders, and you must be told you may stay silent.

      A judge within 72 hours

      Everyone arrested must be brought before a judge within 72 hours, and may not be held incommunicado for more than 48. Neither limit can be suspended.

      Detention is reviewed

      Pretrial detention beyond 30 days needs a judge’s renewal, and may not run past 18 months without a trial.

      Habeas corpus

      Any person may ask a court to test the lawfulness of a detention. The right survives every emergency.

      Confront the evidence

      The accused may confront the witnesses against them and compel witnesses and evidence to be produced in their defence.

      Your own language

      No one may be disadvantaged in public proceedings for using a recognised regional language: French, Filipinic or the Slavic dialects.

      No death penalty

      Capital punishment is permanently abolished. No person shall be executed by the state, and no amendment can bring it back.

      Jury service

      Citizens serve on juries when summoned, subject to lawful exemptions. It is a duty of every citizen under Article 9.

      Articles 18, 22 and 26

      Independent judges

      Judges are chosen for merit, not for loyalty, and can be removed only by impeachment.

      Appointment and tenure

      Chosen by
      The Civil Service Commission, on merit.
      Confirmed by
      The Senate.
      Serves
      Until age 75 or voluntary retirement. Constitutional Court judges serve one twelve-year term.
      Timing
      No appointments in the last six months of a presidential term, unless a death or incapacity creates a vacancy.
      Disclosure
      Financial interests are published every year.
      Barred from
      Practising law, holding any other public office, or taking part in politics.

      Accountability

      Investigated by
      The Control Commission, which can investigate any judge for corruption, abuse of power or gross incompetence.
      Impeachment
      Two thirds of the House to impeach.
      Removal
      A separate two-thirds vote of the Senate, after a trial presided over by the Chief Justice.
      Surveillance
      Only a federal judge may issue a warrant to collect personal data. A senior judge is designated Intelligence Review Judge and may order unlawful data destroyed.
      Report
      An independent judicial panel reviews all warrants each year and a summary is published.

      The oath of judges

      I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully and impartially discharge the duties of my office; that I will uphold and defend the Constitution of the Third Republic against all enemies, foreign and domestic; that I will place the public interest above personal advantage; and that if ever I am ordered to act against the Constitution, I will refuse.

      Articles 27–30

      State courts and the four circuits

      Each of the 26 States has its own constitution, government and court system. Federal appeals are heard in four circuits, each grouping the States of one part of the Federation.

        Articles 49–51

        Beyond the Federation

        The Commonwealth has courts of its own, which hear disputes under the Charter. The Constitution of the Third Republic stays the supreme law in internal governance, individual rights and the structure of the five branches.

        • Disputes between the Federation and Solaria, or Commonwealth institutions, go to the Commonwealth High CourtArt. 51
        • The Federal Assembly is told within seven days of any Commonwealth ruling that affects the rights of Federal citizensArt. 51
        • No Commonwealth institution may require the Federation to break its fundamental rights or its anti-theocracy provisionsArt. 51
        • In a conflict between the Charter and the Constitution, the Commonwealth Constitutional Court decides, after hearing the opinion of this Federation’s Constitutional CourtArt. 49
